Do diet breaks help you lose more fat?

Short answer

In lifters, planned breaks at maintenance produced the same fat loss, with less hunger. In one trial in men with obesity, breaks produced more fat loss per week of dieting. Either way the diet takes longer on the calendar. Breaks are a tool for sticking with a long diet, not a shortcut.

The trial that started the interest

Randomised controlled trial
Intermittent energy restriction improves weight loss efficiency in obese men: the MATADOR study
Byrne NM, Sainsbury A, King NA et al., International Journal of Obesity, 2018
51
men randomised
14.1 vs 9.1 kg
weight lost
30 vs 16
weeks on the calendar

Read it on PubMed →

Both groups did 16 weeks of dieting at two-thirds of maintenance. One group did it straight through. The other alternated two weeks of dieting with two weeks at maintenance, so it took 30 weeks. Among those who finished, the break group lost more weight (14.1 vs 9.1 kg) and more fat (12.3 vs 8.0 kg), and their resting metabolism dropped less than expected for their size. They barely moved during the maintenance blocks.

Who was studied

Men with obesity (average 110 kg, BMI 34), not lifters. The results are for completers only (17 vs 19 men), and the break group needed nearly twice as long. This is “more fat loss per week of dieting”, not “faster fat loss”.

The trial in people who train

Randomised controlled trial
Continuous versus Intermittent Dieting for Fat Loss and Fat-Free Mass Retention in Resistance-trained Adults: The ICECAP Trial
Peos JJ, Helms ER, Fournier PA et al., Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise, 2021
61
trained adults
32
women
No diff.

Read it on PubMed →

Twelve weeks of moderate dieting, either straight through or as four 3-week blocks separated by 1-week breaks at maintenance. Fat loss, fat-free mass, strength, resting metabolism and hormones came out the same. The difference was in how people felt: the break group was less hungry, had less desire to eat, and was more satisfied.

Read it on PubMed →

Looking at one break week up close: fat mass didn't change, scale weight rose about 0.6 kg, leg muscular endurance improved, and hunger and irritability fell while alertness rose. That weight bump is almost certainly glycogen and water from the extra carbohydrate, not fat and not new muscle.

Who was studied

Resistance-trained men and women around 29 years old and roughly 21–25% body fat. This is the population closest to most people reading this. The secondary analysis had no control group for the break week itself, so treat its findings as supportive, not decisive.

How I use them

For a diet longer than about 8–12 weeks, I plan a week at maintenance every few weeks, especially when hunger, sleep or training quality start to slip. Maintenance means maintenance: eat to hold your weight, don't treat it as a free-for-all. Expect the scale to jump a little in the first days and don't let it worry you.

This is not the same thing as fasting-style intermittent eating (5:2, alternate-day fasting). That is a separate question with its own evidence.

What to do

  • Short diet, going well? You don't need breaks.
  • Long diet, or adherence starting to crack? Plan a week at maintenance every 3–6 weeks.
  • Eat at maintenance, not above it. The goal is to hold your weight for the week.
  • Ignore the first-days scale bump. It's water and glycogen.
  • Budget the extra calendar time before you start.

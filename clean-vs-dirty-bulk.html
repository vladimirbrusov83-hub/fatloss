Clean bulk vs dirty bulk: how big should my surplus be?

Short answer

A surplus helps you build muscle. A bigger surplus does not build muscle faster. Muscle growth has a ceiling set by your training and your training age, not by your plate. Roughly 10–20% above maintenance, aiming for 0.25–0.5% of bodyweight per week.

What the research actually shows

Two things are true at once here, and most bulking advice picks one and ignores the other. An energy surplus does help you build muscle. And the amount required is much smaller than the amount most people eat.

The honest headline from this review is a gap, not a number. There is clear evidence that a surplus provides an anabolic stimulus. The authors say so directly, and note it appears even independent of resistance training. But:

  • The specific surplus required is unknown. It has never been validated in a resistance-training population.
  • The textbook figures are back-of-envelope. The familiar "you need X calories to build a kilo of muscle" numbers are derived from the energy stored in the tissue, which ignores the metabolic cost of building it and the body's own adjustments to being overfed.
  • Which is why published guidance ranges so widely. The spread in the recommendations reflects genuine uncertainty, not disagreement about the evidence.

Note the badges on both of these. Neither is a meta-analysis, and this page doesn't pretend otherwise: the pooled evidence on optimal surplus size does not exist. These are the two best syntheses available, and their most valuable contribution is the rate target: advanced lifters should be more conservative on both counts, because the closer you are to your ceiling the smaller the fraction of extra weight that can be muscle.

Who was studied

Neither reference is a trial in a specific population; they synthesise literature drawn largely from young trained and untrained men, with the applied recommendations aimed at natural bodybuilders. The recommendations are reasoned rather than measured. Take the direction seriously and the decimal places lightly.

Why the ceiling is real

Muscle protein synthesis responds to training and to adequate protein. Neither responds to a third helping. Once training, protein, and enough energy to support recovery are in place, the additional calories have exactly one place to go.

The rate ceiling is also strongly tied to training age. A first-year lifter might add 0.5–1 kg of muscle a month. Someone five years in might manage 1–2 kg in a year. The surplus that made sense in year one is a fat-gain protocol in year five, and this is the single most common bulking error: using a beginner's rate at an intermediate's training age.

The real cost of a dirty bulk

The problem isn't aesthetic. It's arithmetic. Gain 10 kg where 3 kg is muscle, and you now need a diet to remove 7 kg of fat, a diet that takes months at a safe rate of loss and during which you'll surrender some of the muscle you just built. The fastest route to being bigger is usually the one that doesn't need undoing.

There's also a diminishing-returns effect: the fatter you get, the worse your body partitions extra calories. Starting a bulk from a leaner point is a better use of the same surplus.

What to do

  • Start with maintenance plus ~10–15%. For most people that's 250–400 calories, not 1,000.
  • Track the rate, not the calories. 0.25–0.5% of bodyweight per week, about 200–400 g for an 80 kg lifter.
  • Adjust from the scale after 3–4 weeks. Gaining faster, eat less; not gaining at all, add 200 calories.
  • Be more conservative the more trained you are. Advanced lifters should sit at the bottom of both ranges.
  • Start bulks from a leaner point, and keep protein at 1.6–2.2 g/kg . See the protein page.

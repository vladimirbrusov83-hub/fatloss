Overstated

Is there a 30 g protein-per-meal cap?

Short answer

There is a point of diminishing returns, but it is a curve, not a wall, and the famous 20–30 g figure comes from studies feeding fast protein on its own. Eat protein with other food and the picture changes. The practical target is ~0.4 g/kg per meal across at least four meals.

Where the number came from

The 20–30 g figure isn't invented. It comes from a real and consistent body of acute research: give someone 20 g of whey and muscle protein synthesis maximises; give 40 g and it doesn't rise much further, while amino acid oxidation does. That's a genuine finding.

The problem is the conditions it was measured under, and what "maximised" was measured on.

  • The ceiling studies used fast-digesting protein without other macronutrients. Whey on an empty stomach empties fast and floods the bloodstream. Slower proteins, and protein eaten with fat and carbohydrate, digest more gradually, which plausibly extends the window over which those amino acids can be used.
  • Higher doses do oxidise more, but not entirely. The review's conclusion is that above ~20 g there is more oxidation, and yet "this is not the fate for all the additional ingested AAs as some are utilized for tissue-building purposes." Diminishing returns, not a hard stop.
  • The practical recommendation is a fraction of bodyweight, not a fixed gram figure. 0.4 g/kg per meal, minimum four meals, to reach at least 1.6 g/kg/day. At the upper end of 2.2 g/kg/day across four meals, that's up to 0.55 g/kg per meal.

Note the badge: this is a review, not a meta-analysis. It's an expert synthesis of acute and long-term studies, and the recommendation is reasoned from them rather than pooled statistically.

Run the numbers and the "cap" looks different. An 80 kg lifter at 0.4 g/kg needs 32 g per meal, already above the folklore figure, and up to 44 g at the upper intake. A 100 kg lifter is at 40–55 g. The fixed 30 g number was always going to be wrong for most people, because it ignored bodyweight entirely.

Who was studied

Predominantly young adults in acute laboratory studies of muscle protein synthesis, using isolated protein doses. Two limitations carry: the acute MPS response measured over hours is a proxy for muscle growth rather than growth itself, and older adults genuinely do need larger per-meal doses to trigger the same response: anabolic resistance is real, and it means these numbers should be revised upward with age.

The measurement problem behind the myth

Muscle protein synthesis measured over 3–5 hours after an isolated protein drink is a narrow window on a slow process. Longer-term whole-body work suggests the body handles large protein doses better than the acute MPS curve implies: excess amino acids aren't simply discarded; they suppress protein breakdown, feed other tissues, and contribute across a longer timeframe.

Which points to the real answer: this is a question of practical distribution, not of a biological ceiling. There's a good reason to spread protein across the day: four solid feedings make 1.6–2.2 g/kg easy to reach, and they're likely a little more efficient than one huge meal. There's no good reason to fear that the 45th gram is wasted.

If your schedule forces big meals

Some people eat twice a day, whether by preference or by shift pattern, or because they're running a compressed eating window. The relevant evidence is reassuring: studies feeding the same daily protein in fewer, larger meals have not shown the muscle loss the cap theory would predict. Four feedings is a reasonable target, not a requirement, and hitting your daily total in two meals beats missing it across five.

What to do

  • Target ~0.4 g/kg per meal. 32 g at 80 kg, 40 g at 100 kg. Scale it to you, don't use a fixed 30.
  • Aim for four protein feedings a day. Enough to reach 1.6–2.2 g/kg without a scramble.
  • Stop discarding protein above 30 g. A 60 g steak-sized serving is not wasted.
  • Add 25–50% per meal if you're over 60. Anabolic resistance means older muscle needs a bigger trigger.
  • Prioritise the daily total over the split. See the protein page for the number that actually matters.

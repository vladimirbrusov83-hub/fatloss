Overstated

Is fasted cardio better for fat loss?

Short answer

Fasted training does burn more fat during the session, and that part is real and measurable. What no one has shown is that it makes you leaner, because fat burned in an hour and fat lost over a week are different quantities. And for long sessions, eating first makes you perform better.

What the research actually shows

Start with the one thing everyone gets right: train without eating and you will oxidise a higher proportion of fat during the session. That is not in dispute. Where it goes wrong is the assumption that this is the same measurement as losing body fat.

Systematic review and meta-analysis
Effects of fasted vs fed-state exercise on performance and post-exercise metabolism: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Aird TP, Davies RW, Carson BP, Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports, 2018
46
studies
2
outcomes: performance and metabolism

Read it on PubMed →

  • Eating first improved prolonged aerobic performance (p = 0.012). For shorter sessions it made no difference (p = 0.687). If your session is long, fasting costs you output.
  • Fasted exercise raised post-exercise circulating free fatty acids (p = 0.023) and left intact some signalling in muscle and fat tissue that pre-exercise feeding blunts: pathways involved in mitochondrial adaptation and fuel selection.
  • The review's own conclusion is that more research is needed to establish what these acute differences add up to chronically.

Read that list again for what it doesn't contain: body fat. This review is about performance and acute metabolic signalling. It is the strongest pooled evidence on fasted training, and it does not report that fasted training makes you leaner, because the trials in it were not designed to answer that.

Be suspicious of anyone citing "fasted cardio burns more fat" as a fat-loss claim. It is a substrate-utilisation finding wearing a body-composition costume.

Who was studied

A broad mix across 46 studies: mostly endurance-oriented protocols in recreationally active and trained adults, mostly acute single sessions rather than long training blocks. That acute-versus-chronic gap is the entire limitation: a study measuring what you burn in 60 minutes cannot tell you what you weigh in 12 weeks.

Why burning fat and losing fat come apart

Your body settles its fuel accounts over about 24 hours, not over one session. Burn a greater share of fat during a fasted morning run and you will oxidise correspondingly more carbohydrate later in the day, once you've eaten. The session-level difference is real and the 24-hour balance largely absorbs it. What determines whether the fat comes off is whether you finished the day in a deficit, which is the actual mechanism.

So how should you choose?

On whichever one you'll do consistently and perform well in. Genuine considerations:

  • Long or hard sessions: eat first. This is the review's clearest finding. Anything over about an hour, or intervals, benefits from fuel.
  • Easy 30–45 minute sessions: whatever you prefer. No performance cost either way.
  • Some people feel sick training fed, some feel flat training fasted. This is a legitimate deciding factor, and it's the one most likely to affect whether you show up.
  • If you lift fasted, that's a different question. The timing evidence says the day's total protein matters more, but a 10-hour overnight fast plus a hard session is a long time unfed.

What to do

  • Pick fasted or fed on comfort and consistency. Neither has been shown to change fat loss.
  • Eat before long or high-intensity sessions. The pooled data supports better performance, and better sessions are worth more than fuel-mix trivia.
  • Judge fat loss by the week, not the session. Weekly weight trend and the mirror, not what your watch says you burned.
  • Set the deficit properly. That's the variable that decides this.
  • Don't lift heavy fasted just for the fat-burning. There's no fat-loss payoff, and there is a performance cost.

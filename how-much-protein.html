How much protein do I need to build muscle?

Short answer

About 1.6 grams per kilogram of bodyweight per day. That is where the pooled evidence stops improving. Eating more isn't dangerous and can help while dieting; it just stops adding muscle.

What the research actually shows

This is the rare nutrition question with a clean answer, because someone pooled every decent trial and looked for the point where the benefit stopped.

Meta-analysis
A systematic review, meta-analysis and meta-regression of the effect of protein supplementation on resistance training-induced gains in muscle mass and strength in healthy adults
Morton RW et al., British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2018
49
randomised trials
1,863
participants
1.6
g/kg/day breakpoint

Three findings matter for you:

  • The ceiling is around 1.6 g/kg/day. Above that, extra protein produced no further gain in fat-free mass. The curve flattens; it does not turn downward.
  • The total effect of supplementing is modest. Averaged across all trials, extra protein added 0.30 kg of fat-free mass (95% CI 0.09–0.52) and about 2.5 kg to one-rep max. Protein is a requirement, not a growth drug; the training does the work.
  • It works better if you already lift, and worse as you age. The benefit was larger in resistance-trained subjects and shrank with increasing age. This is the unusual case where the finding transfers to trained lifters rather than away from them.

A second, larger review looked at the shape of the curve rather than a single cut-off, and found the same thing from a different angle.

Systematic review
Dose-response relationship between protein intake and muscle mass increase: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Tagawa R et al., Nutrition Reviews, 2020

5,402

Below about 1.3 g/kg/day, each extra 0.1 g/kg bought roughly 0.39 kg of lean mass. Above it, the same increment bought only 0.12 kg. Same story: steep returns while you're under-eating protein, shallow returns once you're not.

Who was studied

Mixed: untrained and trained adults across both reviews, but the meta-regression specifically tested training status and found the effect was larger in trained lifters. That makes this one of the few protein numbers you can quote at a lifter without a caveat. The age effect is real though: an older trainee gets less out of the same gram of protein.

So why do you hear 2 g per pound?

Because it's harmless, it sounds serious, and nobody ever got fired for over-recommending protein. Roughly 2.2 g/kg (1 g per pound of bodyweight) sits comfortably above the breakpoint, which is why it survives as a rule of thumb. It works by being generously wrong in the safe direction.

There is one situation where deliberately overshooting earns its keep: an aggressive fat-loss phase. In a deficit, higher protein protects lean mass and blunts hunger, and intakes toward 2.2–2.4 g/kg are commonly used by lean athletes cutting hard (Helms 2014). That's a different job than building muscle, and it's why the numbers you hear from bodybuilders in contest prep look inflated compared to the meta-analysis.

If you're worried about the high end: 28 controlled trials found no effect on kidney function in healthy adults.

What to do

  • Target 1.6 g/kg/day, about 0.73 g per pound. For an 80 kg lifter that's roughly 130 g.
  • Going to 2.2 g/kg is fine, just don't expect more muscle for it. Use the high end while dieting, not while bulking.
  • Use bodyweight, not goal weight, unless you're carrying a lot of fat. Then use an estimate of your lean mass or goal weight instead, or you'll chase a number you don't need.
  • Hit the daily total before you optimise anything else. Distribution, timing, and source are all downstream of this number . See the 30 g myth and the anabolic window.

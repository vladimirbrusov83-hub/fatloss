Can I build muscle and lose fat at the same time?

Short answer

Yes, if you are new to lifting, returning after a layoff, or carrying a fair amount of fat. If you are lean and trained, it becomes so slow that it is barely worth calling a strategy. There is no meta-analysis on this question, so treat every confident number you read, including these, with more caution than usual.

Start with what isn't known

Every other page on this site leads with pooled evidence. This one can't. Searching PubMed for a systematic review or meta-analysis on body recomposition returns nothing. The trials are too few, too short, and too varied in population to pool. So what follows is individual studies, and the appropriate confidence is lower.

That matters because recomposition is exactly the topic where confident numbers get invented. Anyone who tells you how many grams of muscle you can add per week in a deficit is extrapolating from studies like the two below.

What the research actually shows

A deliberately brutal design: a 40% energy deficit, resistance training plus high-intensity intervals six days a week, for four weeks. One group ate 1.2 g/kg protein, the other 2.4 g/kg.

  • High-protein group: +1.2 kg lean mass, −4.8 kg fat.
  • Low-protein group: +0.1 kg lean mass, −3.5 kg fat.
  • Both differences were statistically significant, and body composition was measured with a four-compartment model, the good method, not a bathroom scale.

Gaining muscle in a 40% deficit sounds impossible, and it is genuinely remarkable. It also comes with the largest caveat on this page, in the "who was studied" box below.

The second trial approaches it from the opposite end: already-lean athletes, gentle deficit.

Elite athletes losing 0.7% of bodyweight a week gained 2.1% lean body mass while losing fat. The fast-loss group (1.4%/week) gained none. So recomposition happened even in trained athletes, but only at the slow rate, and the magnitude is far more modest than Longland's.

Who was studied

This is the caveat that governs the whole page. Longland's participants were young men described as recreationally active, not trained lifters, on a four-week supervised intervention with provided diets. Newcomers to structured resistance training have an enormous, temporary capacity to add muscle in almost any circumstance, and four weeks is short enough that some of the measured lean mass gain will be glycogen and water rather than contractile tissue. Garthe's athletes were genuinely trained, and their gain was correspondingly smaller. Take the pattern from these studies; do not take Longland's numbers as your forecast.

Who can actually recomp

The honest ranking, from most to least likely:

  • Beginners. First year of proper training. Recomposition here is close to automatic: expect it rather than plan for it.
  • Returners. Rebuilding muscle you previously had is far faster than building it the first time. Months, not years.
  • People with substantial fat to lose. Plenty of stored energy to fund muscle repair, so the deficit bites less.
  • Anyone who's been training badly. If your programme has been unstructured, fixing the training alone can produce recomposition.
  • Lean, trained lifters. Technically possible, practically marginal. If you're under 15% body fat with years of consistent training, you'll make faster progress running a proper bulk and a proper cut than trying to do both at once.

The conditions that make it possible

Every study where recomposition occurred had the same three things. They aren't optional extras:

  • High protein. Longland's effect was the protein: 2.4 g/kg versus 1.2. In a deficit, aim toward the top of the usual range, 2.0–2.4 g/kg.
  • Hard resistance training with progression. The deficit tells your body to shed tissue; the training tells it which tissue to keep. No stimulus, no recomposition.
  • A moderate deficit, unless you're a beginner. Longland got away with 40% because the participants were new and supervised. For a trained lifter, Garthe's slow approach is the model.

How to tell it's working

Scale weight is useless here by definition; that's the whole point of recomposition. Track strength in the gym (going up in a deficit is the clearest sign muscle is being kept or built), waist circumference, and photos every four weeks in the same light. Expect it to be slow, and expect the scale to sit still for weeks while things change underneath it.

What to do

  • Be honest about which group you're in. Beginner or returner: recomp away. Lean and trained: run separate phases instead.
  • Push protein to 2.0–2.4 g/kg/day. This is the variable that produced the effect in the strongest trial here.
  • Use a small deficit of 10–20%. Aggressive deficits only worked in beginners under supervision.
  • Train hard and progress the load. Recomposition is a training outcome that nutrition permits, not the other way round.
  • Measure with strength, waist and photos. Give it 8–12 weeks before judging, and ignore the scale.

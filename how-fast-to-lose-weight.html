How fast can I lose weight without losing muscle?

Short answer

Around 0.5–1% of bodyweight per week, and the low end of that is better than the high end. In the one good randomised comparison, athletes losing 0.7% a week gained lean mass; athletes losing 1.4% a week gained none.

What the research actually shows

Most rate-of-loss advice is asserted rather than tested. This one was tested, in exactly the population you'd want: athletes who lift, with strength and power measured before and after.

Both groups lifted four times a week and both lost the same total weight, about 5.6% of bodyweight. The slow group just took longer to do it (8.5 weeks versus 5.3). The results diverged sharply:

  • Lean body mass rose 2.1% in the slow group and was flat in the fast group (−0.2%). Significant difference between groups.
  • Strength favoured the slow group. 1RM improvements were better with the gentler deficit.
  • The calorie difference was 19% versus 30% below maintenance. That's the practical translation: the aggressive arm wasn't a crash diet, just a normal-looking aggressive cut.

Read that first bullet again. The slow group didn't merely preserve lean mass while dieting; it added some. That's the argument for patience in one number.

Note the study design badge. This is a narrative review: expert synthesis, not pooled data. It's useful for practical structure, and it is not evidence in the same sense the trial above is.

Who was studied

Elite athletes with a mean age of ~23, already lean, already resistance training four times a week. This is one of the rare studies whose population is more trained than you. That direction of mismatch is favourable: if a lean elite athlete loses lean mass cutting fast, someone with more fat to spare has more buffer, not less. Sample size was small (24), and the intervention ran 5–9 weeks, not months.

Adjusting the number for yourself

The 0.5–1% range is a range for a reason:

  • Carrying a lot of fat? Use the top of the range or slightly above at first. More fat mass means more energy available and less pressure on muscle tissue.
  • Already lean and chasing the last few kilos? Use the bottom, or slower. This is where lean mass gets expensive, and where recomposition stops being realistic.
  • New to lifting? You get an unusual grace period: beginners can gain muscle in a deficit that a trained lifter can't.

A worked example: 90 kg puts 0.5–1% at 450–900 g a week. Aim for the middle, roughly 700 g. At 60 kg it's 300–600 g, and you should sit near the bottom.

What protects muscle while the weight comes off

Rate is one of three levers, and the other two aren't optional. Protein at the higher end, 1.6–2.2 g/kg, and continued heavy lifting, because the stimulus to keep muscle is what tells the body which tissue to spare. Cutting your training volume to "save energy for cardio" during a diet is the reliable way to lose the muscle you were trying to protect.

What to do

  • Target 0.5–1% of bodyweight per week. Leaner means slower.
  • Keep protein at 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day. The high end of the range earns its keep in a deficit.
  • Keep lifting heavy. Maintain intensity even if you trim volume; that's the signal that protects muscle.
  • Use a weekly average, not daily weights. Day-to-day swings are water and food in transit.
  • If you're losing faster than 1% a week, eat more. You are not ahead of schedule; you're paying in lean mass.
